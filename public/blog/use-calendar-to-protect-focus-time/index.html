How to Use Your Calendar to Protect Focus Time

2 October 2026 5 min read

Your calendar only protects focus time if focus time is on it. A block called "Write proposal, 9:30–11:00" gets defended. An empty gap gets filled by whoever asks first.

Treat your calendar as a record of what you've committed to, not just a list of what other people have asked of you, and it becomes the best focus tool you already own. The method has three moves: block the work by name, colour-code by energy, and slice each block into Pomodoros. Setup takes about fifteen minutes and needs no new software.

Why a calendar usually makes focus worse

By default, a calendar shows other people's demands. Meetings appear because someone sent an invite. Your own work appears nowhere, so the rest of the day looks empty to everyone, including you.

Meanwhile your todo list holds 40 items with no time attached. The list says what, the calendar says when, and the two never meet. You open the day, see three meetings and a wall of tasks, and do the real work in scraps between calls.

Those scraps cost more than their length suggests. Sophie Leroy's research on "attention residue" found that part of your attention stays on the previous task after you switch. A 25-minute gap between meetings isn't 25 minutes of focus. It's closer to 10, once you've stopped thinking about the last call and started worrying about the next.

The calendar isn't the problem. It's a system that only knows about other people's commitments.

The method: three moves

1. Block time for named tasks, not "focus time". Pick today's tasks first, and keep it to five at most. Then give each one a slot on the calendar, titled with the actual deliverable: "Draft intro section" or "Reconcile September invoices". A vague block called "Deep work" is easy to drop when a meeting request lands. A block with a named deliverable is harder to trade away.

Set these blocks to "busy", not "free". If your calendar lets you auto-decline conflicting invites during them, switch that on.

2. Colour-code by energy, not by project. Use three colours. One is for work that needs your best thinking: writing, analysis, design, hard problems. One is for medium work like email, admin and review. One is for low-energy work, including meetings where you're mostly listening.

Look at the week and check the pattern. If every high-energy block sits at 3pm and you're sharpest at 9am, you've found a mismatch you can fix in two drags of the mouse. Spend a week noticing when work actually flows before you rearrange everything. Most people's guess about their best hours is only roughly right.

3. Cut each block into Pomodoros. A 90-minute block fits three 25-minute Pomodoros with 5-minute breaks between them. The Pomodoro Technique, created by Francesco Cirillo, gives the block a structure inside it, so you aren't staring at a vague hour and a half. Decide what you'll do in the first 25 minutes before you start. It's worth planning the breaks too. What you do on a Pomodoro break decides whether the next block starts sharp or sluggish.

Where this breaks down

Your calendar is already full. If back-to-back meetings leave no gaps, blocking won't conjure time. You have to cut or move something first. Finding deep work time on a full calendar covers how to negotiate that. The meeting cost calculator in FocusShield also helps. Seeing a recurring one-hour meeting priced in salary-hours makes the "do we need this?" conversation easier.

Your job is interruption-driven. Support, ops and some management roles can't protect 90 minutes. Use shorter blocks of 25 to 50 minutes and accept that some will be broken. A block that gets interrupted can be moved. It doesn't have to be deleted.

You over-schedule. If you fill every hour, the plan will be wrecked by lunchtime and you'll stop trusting it. Leave about a third of the day unblocked for overruns and the things that turn up unannounced. That's also the most common reason people drop a system. The Pomodoro mistake that ends streaks by day three is closely related.

Colleagues ignore your blocks. Some will book over them anyway. Keep the block visible and say once, plainly, that you're unavailable then and free after. Most people respect a named task more than a generic "focus" label.

One thing to do today

Open tomorrow's calendar and find the longest stretch without a meeting. Pick the one task that matters most and put it in that slot, titled with the deliverable. Set it to busy.

That's all. Don't colour-code the whole week or rebuild your routine. Make one block, then see tomorrow whether protecting it was easier than you expected.
