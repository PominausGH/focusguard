How do I pick 5 tasks when everything feels urgent?

23 October 2026 5 min read

Write everything down, then ask one question of each item: "What actually happens if this slips till tomorrow?" Anything where the honest answer is "nothing much" comes off today's list. Whatever has a real consequence stays, and you cut that group down to five.

That's the whole method. The rest of this article covers how to run it in about ten minutes, what to do when other people's urgency keeps overriding yours, and how to handle the day when you genuinely have twelve real things.

Why everything feels urgent

Most to-do lists mix three different things: tasks that matter, tasks that are loud, and tasks that are just old. They all look identical on the page, so your brain treats them as equally pressing. Hence the low-grade panic.

The usual fix is to sort the list better. Colour codes, priority tags, matrices. But sorting a 40-item list is still a 40-item list. You spend your best energy of the day on admin about the work, and the list is still there tomorrow.

There's also a quieter problem. Urgency is contagious and mostly external. A Slack message marked "quick question," an email with a red flag, a manager who says "when you get a sec." None of these tell you how much is at stake. They tell you how recently someone else remembered something.

If you've abandoned a priority system before, this is probably why. It asked you to rank everything, and ranking everything is the thing you couldn't do. A limit works better than a ranking because it forces the decision up front.

The five-task triage, step by step

1. Dump it all out (3 minutes). Open a blank page and write down everything competing for your attention. Work tasks, half-promised favours, the invoice you keep avoiding. Don't sort or edit. The point is to get it out of your head so you can see its actual size.

2. Ask the slip question (5 minutes). Go through each item and ask: "What happens if this slips till tomorrow?" Be specific about the consequence. "Someone is slightly annoyed" is different from "we miss a contractual deadline" or "the client can't start their part."

Sort the answers into three piles. Real consequence tomorrow: a deadline, a blocked person, money lost. Mild consequence: awkward but survivable. No consequence: it only feels urgent.

3. Cut to five (2 minutes). Take the first pile. If it has five or fewer items, you're done. If it has more, rank by two things: which have the hardest deadline, and which only you can do. Tasks someone else could do are candidates for delegating or declining.

4. Park the rest somewhere boring. Everything that didn't make the cut goes into a "later" note you won't look at until tomorrow's triage. It isn't deleted and it isn't today's problem.

5. Work in timed blocks. Use the Pomodoro Technique: 25 minutes on one task, then a 5-minute break. Five tasks is a realistic day for most people because a task often takes more than one block.

When other people's urgency overrides yours

This is the honest weak point. You can triage perfectly at 8:30am and have it wrecked by 9:15 when your manager drops something on you.

The cap helps here, because it makes the trade-off visible. When a new request arrives, you can't just add it. You have to ask what comes off. Saying "I can do that today, but it means the report moves to tomorrow. Is that okay?" is far harder to wave away than "I'm swamped."

Sometimes the answer will be "yes, swap it." That's fine. You made the call on purpose, and the swap is a choice rather than a pile-up.

"But I have 12 real things"

Sometimes you do. A launch week, a month-end close, an exam period. The temptation is to rebuild the giant list and promise yourself you'll get to it all.

Don't. Instead, treat the twelve as a queue and keep today's list at five. Pick the five with the hardest deadlines, and tomorrow, run the triage again. Some of the twelve will have resolved themselves, been reassigned, or been quietly dropped. Some will have moved up. A fresh daily cut handles this better than a master plan that's out of date by lunchtime.

If five really isn't enough, check whether any task is secretly three tasks. "Prepare presentation" is research, slides and rehearsal. Splitting it makes your list look bigger, but it also makes it honest.

Where this breaks down

If your job is mostly reactive, such as support, on-call or an open-door manager, you can't plan five tasks and expect to protect them. In that case, make the list smaller (two or three) and treat it as the minimum you want to get done between interruptions.

One thing to do today

Set a timer for ten minutes. Write down everything on your mind, then put "slips till tomorrow: what happens?" next to each item and answer honestly. Circle five. Cross out nothing else, just move it to a separate page.

Then start a 25-minute block on the first circled item before you do anything else. You've now made the hardest decision of the day, so the rest is just doing the work.
