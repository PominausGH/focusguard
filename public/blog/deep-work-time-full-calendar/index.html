How to Find Deep Work Time When Your Calendar Is Full

11 September 2026 5 min read

If your calendar is back-to-back, deep work isn't going to appear in a gap — there isn't one. You have to create it, which almost always means moving something rather than finding something. That's the actual work: not time management, but a series of small, slightly uncomfortable trade-offs about what gets bumped.

Most advice about "finding time" assumes you're looking for something that's already there, hiding between meetings. It isn't. If your week is full, every hour is full because you or someone else decided it should be. Getting deep work time back means deciding, on purpose, that something else matters less this week.

Why "just block your calendar" usually fails

The standard advice is to block out two hours for deep work. Plenty of people try this and it lasts about a week. The block gets meeting-requested over, or a colleague pings with something "quick," or the block sits there unused because nothing about it felt different from free time.

The real failure mode isn't a lack of blocks — it's that the block has no weight. A calendar entry that says "Focus Time" carries less authority than a meeting invite from your manager, so it loses every time there's a conflict. It also assumes willpower will show up to defend it, which is exactly the thing that's already in short supply by 2pm on a Tuesday.

Cal Newport's argument in Deep Work is that protected time only holds if it's treated as a real commitment, not a suggestion to yourself. That means the block needs a reason other people can see, a task attached to it, and a default answer ready for when someone tries to book over it.

A practical way to actually carve out the time

Start smaller than you think. Instead of trying to protect two hours, protect one 25-minute block a day for two weeks. That's a single Pomodoro — short enough that it survives contact with a busy week, long enough to move a real task forward.

Here's the sequence that tends to hold up:

1. Audit one week of your actual calendar. Not what you think it looks like — open it and count. Most people find two or three recurring meetings that don't need them present the whole time, or at all. That's where the first block comes from.

2. Put the block where a meeting used to be, not where a meeting doesn't yet exist. An empty 9am slot gets claimed by the next person who needs a meeting. A slot that used to hold a status update you no longer need to attend is yours by default.

3. Name the block and give it a task. "Focus Time" invites negotiation. "Client proposal — draft" doesn't. Attach one specific task before the block starts, so there's no five minutes lost deciding what to work on once you sit down.

5. Set a boundary line, and say it out loud once. "I'm heads-down 9–9:30, back on Slack after" takes one sentence in a team channel. Most people never say it and then wonder why nobody respects a boundary they never stated.

Where this breaks down

If your job is genuinely reactive — support, on-call, executive assistant work — a fixed daily block isn't realistic, and forcing one will just create guilt. In that case, protect the block opportunistically: the moment a gap opens, use it immediately rather than waiting for a scheduled one.

It also breaks down if you don't have calendar authority — junior roles, client-facing work, some agency jobs. There, the move isn't to block time unilaterally but to ask your manager which meetings you can skip. Framed as "which of these needs me in the room," most managers will cut at least one.

And it won't survive a single "urgent" request that gets treated as more urgent than it is. Twenty-five minutes of silence rarely breaks anything — most things marked urgent can wait half an hour, and treating them as unable to wait is usually a habit, not a fact.

One thing to do today

Open tomorrow's calendar and find one 25-minute gap — a meeting that could be 25 minutes shorter, or one you don't need to attend at all. Book a single focused block in its place, name it after the actual task, and run one Pomodoro against it. Don't plan the whole week yet. Just protect the one block and see if it holds.
